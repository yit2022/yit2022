*By Isaac Muthui, CEO, Tamalaki Business Network Ltd. Originally published at [factoraerp.com](https://factoraerp.com/en/blog/environmental-monitoring-checklist-food-factories-kenya).*

Most food factories test their product. Far fewer test the room the product is made in. A batch of yoghurt, cooked sausage, bread, peanut butter or juice can pass every finished-product test and still be packed next to a drain, a conveyor joint or a cold-room floor that has been quietly harbouring bacteria for months.

That is what an environmental monitoring programme is for. It is a planned routine of swabbing and sampling surfaces, drains, equipment and air in the plant, testing those samples for target organisms, and acting on what you find. Done well, it tells you where contamination lives before it reaches a pack. Done badly, it is a handful of swabs taken from the cleanest stainless steel in the building, all negative, filed and forgotten.

This environmental monitoring checklist for food factories is written for dairies, meat processors, bakeries, juice, snack and ready-to-eat food plants and other Kenyan and East African food and agro factories. It does not replace your HACCP team, your microbiologist, your laboratory or your certification body. It sets out ten practical checks for building a programme that finds problems instead of hiding them.

## What the standards actually say

**Codex.** The [Codex General Principles of Food Hygiene, CXC 1-1969](https://doi.org/10.4060/cc6125en), says that measures should be taken "for sampling and testing the environment and food contact surfaces (e.g. protein and allergen test swabs, or microbiological testing for indicator organisms) to help verify that cleaning and disinfection programmes are effective and being applied properly". In its HACCP chapter, it lists "sampling and testing the environment for microbial contaminants and their indicators, such as Listeria" as a verification activity, and says verification "should be carried out by someone other than the person who is responsible for performing the monitoring and corrective actions".

**Codex on Listeria.** The [Codex Guidelines on the Application of General Principles of Food Hygiene to the Control of Listeria monocytogenes in Foods, CXG 61-2007](https://www.fao.org/input/download/standards/10740/CXG_061e.pdf), is the most detailed public guide. It says "an effective environmental monitoring program is an essential component of a Listeria control program", and that the need for one "is highest for ready-to-eat foods that support L. monocytogenes growth and that are not given a post-packaging listericidal treatment". Its Annex I lists the factors to consider: type of product and process, type of samples (food contact and non-food contact surfaces), target organisms, sampling locations and number of samples, frequency, sampling tools, analytical methods, data management and actions on positive results. It notes that "environmental monitoring is not to be confused with monitoring as defined in the HACCP". It also says L. monocytogenes "can become established and persist in floor drains", and that "generally manufacturers should expect to find them occasionally in the processing environment".

**The East African code.** [EAS 39:2001, the East African code of practice for hygiene in the food and drink manufacturing industry](https://law.resource.org/pub/eac/ibr/eas.39.2001.pdf), makes environmental sampling a requirement: "Environmental sampling and microbiological analysis of these samples shall be carried out, at a level commensurate with identifying the risk to the safety of the finished product" (4.3.4). When results are outside the action limits "for raw materials, in process materials, finished product or the environment, timely action shall be taken to identify and rectify the cause" (4.3.7). It recommends a tracking system for microbiological contamination on each production line "to track the location of possible sources of contamination", with sampling increased "if problems are encountered until effective action has been taken" (4.6.2). It asks for cleaning to be checked "by microbiological analysis of rinse water, swabbing of surfaces, or other appropriate means" (6.3.3), says that after cleaning food contact surfaces "should have a TVC of less than one colony per square centimetre" (6.4.5), and lists "records of environmental tests" among the records to keep (14.3.4).

**Kenya's food hygiene regulations.** The [Food, Drugs and Chemical Substances (Food Hygiene) Regulations](https://faolex.fao.org/docs/pdf/ken106527.pdf) made under Cap. 254 require that "chemical, micro-biological or extraneous material testing procedures shall be utilised where necessary to identify sanitation failures or food contamination", and that contaminated food and ingredients be rejected or adequately treated (Second Schedule).

**A useful benchmark.** US rules are not binding in Kenya, but they are precise. [21 CFR 117.165](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-117/subpart-C/section-117.165) requires environmental monitoring "if contamination of a ready-to-eat food with an environmental pathogen is a hazard requiring a preventive control". The written procedure must be scientifically valid, identify the test microorganisms, the sampling locations and number of sites, the timing and frequency, the tests and methods, the laboratory, and the corrective action procedures. [21 CFR 117.3](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-117/subpart-A/section-117.3) defines an environmental pathogen as one "capable of surviving and persisting within the manufacturing, processing, packing, or holding environment", naming Listeria monocytogenes and Salmonella spp. as examples.

## Why environmental monitoring goes wrong

Environmental monitoring rarely fails because a factory takes no swabs. It fails because the swabs are designed to pass.

The patterns are familiar. Samples are taken straight after cleaning and sanitising, from the same few shiny surfaces, every time. Drains, door seals, cold-room floors, conveyor undersides and cleaning tools are never sampled. The schedule says "monthly" but slips to "when the auditor is due". Results come back on paper and go into a file, so nobody sees that the same filler area has been borderline for six months. A positive result triggers one extra clean and a re-swab, and when that is negative the matter is closed without anyone asking where the organism came from. And because nobody agreed in advance what a positive means, the decision about product on the floor is made under pressure.

## The 10-step environmental monitoring checklist for food factories

### 1. Decide where you need a programme and what it is for

- Start from your hazard analysis. The need is greatest where ready-to-eat food is exposed to the environment after a kill step (pasteurisation, cooking, baking) and before it is sealed, and where the food supports the growth of pathogens. Codex CXG 61 says products that are not further exposed to the environment after in-pack treatment may not necessarily need it.
- Write down the purpose: verifying cleaning, finding and eliminating harbourage sites, or verifying that a preventive control for an environmental pathogen works. The purpose drives everything else.
- Our [HACCP CCP monitoring checklist](https://factoraerp.com/en/blog/haccp-ccp-monitoring-checklist-food-factories-kenya) explains the difference between CCP monitoring and verification. Environmental monitoring is verification.

### 2. Map the plant and divide it into zones

- Draw the site plan and mark the hygiene areas: raw, high-care or ready-to-eat, packing, storage and support areas.
- A common way to organise sampling sites is by zones, from food contact surfaces in the exposed ready-to-eat area (zone 1), through non-food contact surfaces right next to them (zone 2), to surfaces further away in the same room such as floors, drains and walls (zone 3), and areas outside the processing room such as corridors, changing rooms and stores (zone 4). Codex asks you to sample both food contact and non-food contact surfaces.
- Keep the map with the programme and update it when the layout changes.

### 3. Choose target organisms and indicators

- Agree with your microbiologist or laboratory what you are testing for and why. CXG 61 notes that Listeria spp. are "a good indicator of conditions supporting the potential presence" of L. monocytogenes. In dry or low-moisture plants such as millers, snack and powder plants, Salmonella is often the organism of concern. Hygiene indicators such as total viable count, Enterobacteriaceae, or rapid ATP or protein swabs are useful for cleaning verification.
- Do not mix up the two jobs. A rapid hygiene swab tells you whether a surface was cleaned. A pathogen or indicator test on drains and floors tells you whether the environment is harbouring something. You need both, for different reasons.

### 4. Pick sampling sites that could find a problem

- Choose the places where organisms survive: drains, floor-wall junctions, cold-room floors and condensers, conveyor rollers and undersides, door seals, cracked or worn equipment, hollow frames, cleaning tools, foot dips and the wheels of trolleys that move between areas. CXG 61 says sampling locations should be reviewed regularly.
- Add sites after maintenance, construction or new equipment, as Codex recommends. Our [preventive maintenance guide](https://factoraerp.com/en/blog/preventive-maintenance-basics-food-agro-factories-kenya) covers how to plan around that.
- Give every site a code on the map so results can be trended by location.

### 5. Set a frequency based on risk and history

- Sample food contact surfaces in exposed ready-to-eat areas more often than walls in a store. CXG 61 says frequency should be based on the product, the process and existing data, and that where there is no data you should generate enough to set the frequency properly.
- Rotate sites so that, over a cycle, every site on the map gets sampled.
- Increase frequency after a positive result, as both CXG 61 and EAS 39 recommend, until the cause has been fixed.

### 6. Sample at the right time, not only after cleaning

- Swabs taken immediately after sanitising tell you about the sanitiser, not about the environment. For finding harbourage sites, sample during production or a few hours into the shift, and occasionally before the start-up clean.
- Use tools that suit the surface. CXG 61 suggests sponges for large flat surfaces, swabs for cracks and crevices and scrapers for hard residues.
- The sampler must not become the source. Use clean gloves, sterile tools and a set route from cleaner to dirtier areas. EAS 39 asks for sampling utensils to be stored in a suitable sterilant solution. Our [personal hygiene checklist](https://factoraerp.com/en/blog/personal-hygiene-staff-health-checklist-food-factories-kenya) covers the basics.

### 7. Use a competent laboratory and suitable methods

- Use methods able to detect the target organisms in environmental samples. Codex says this should be documented. Record which laboratory tests what, by which method.
- If samples are pooled to save cost, agree in advance how a positive pool will be traced back to individual sites, because CXG 61 says further testing will be needed to locate it.
- Transport samples to the laboratory in the conditions it specifies, and record when they were taken and received.

### 8. Write the response plan before the first positive

- Define, in advance, what each kind of result triggers. CXG 61 describes a range from no action to "intensified cleaning, to source tracing (increased environmental testing), to review of hygienic practices up to holding and testing of product".
- A positive on a food contact surface in an exposed ready-to-eat area should normally trigger a review of the product made since the last negative result. Put that product on [QA hold](https://factoraerp.com/en/blog/qa-hold-checklist-before-batch-leaves-warehouse-kenya) until it has been assessed. EAS 39 requires a written procedure so that product made during a control failure is not released until it is shown to be safe.
- Investigate the cause, not just the site: what is above it, what drains into it, what moves through it. Record the root cause and the corrective action, and re-sample the site and the sites around it. If product has left the plant, your [mock recall](https://factoraerp.com/en/blog/how-to-run-a-mock-recall-food-traceability-kenya) practice is what makes the next hour work.

### 9. Control drains, cleaning tools and traffic

- Clean drains in a way that does not spread contamination. CXG 61 says drains should not be cleaned during production, high-pressure hoses should not be used to clear them, drain-cleaning utensils should be dedicated and easily distinguishable, and people who clean drains should change and wash before touching food contact surfaces.
- Keep cleaning equipment dedicated to raw or finished areas and colour-coded, as both CXG 61 and EAS 39 suggest. EAS 39 also points out that Listeria tends to be carried in on footwear and that brushes can then spread it onto the plant.
- Our [cleaning and sanitation checklist](https://factoraerp.com/en/blog/cleaning-sanitation-checklist-food-factories-kenya) and [water quality checklist](https://factoraerp.com/en/blog/water-quality-checklist-food-factories-kenya) cover the programmes environmental monitoring is checking.

### 10. Record, trend and review the results

- Record every sample: site code, zone, date and time, before or after cleaning, sampler, organism tested, method, laboratory, result and action taken. EAS 39 lists records of environmental tests among the records to keep. See our [food safety records checklist](https://factoraerp.com/en/blog/food-safety-records-checklist-food-factories-kenya).
- Trend results by site, zone, line and month. CXG 61 says a long-term review "can also reveal low level, intermittent contamination that may otherwise go unnoticed".
- Review the programme itself at least yearly, and as part of your [internal audits](https://factoraerp.com/en/blog/internal-audit-checklist-food-factories-kenya). A programme that has not found anything in a year may be sampling the wrong places.

## A simple sampling plan register

| Column | What to record |
|---|---|
| Site code | e.g. RTE-Z1-03 (filler nozzle area), RTE-Z3-01 (drain by packing line) |
| Zone | 1 to 4, or your own hygiene area names |
| Target | e.g. Listeria spp., Salmonella, Enterobacteriaceae, TVC, ATP |
| Method and tool | Swab, sponge, rinse water, settle plate; lab method |
| Frequency | e.g. weekly, monthly, rotating |
| Timing | During production, before start-up, after cleaning |
| Action limit and response | What a result above the limit triggers, and who decides |
| Owner | Person responsible for sampling and for follow-up |

If you cannot fill in the response column, the programme is not ready.

## An illustration

*This is an illustrative example, not a real case.* A small dairy making yoghurt swabs the filler nozzles once a month, straight after the sanitising step, and every result is negative. When it redraws its plan to include the drain under the filler, the cold-room floor and the trolley wheels, sampled mid-shift, the drain returns a Listeria spp. positive. Because the response plan is written down, the team knows what to do: intensified drain cleaning with dedicated tools, extra sampling of the sites around the drain, a review of the product made since the last clear result, and a look at what drains into that point. The positive is not a failure of the programme. It is the programme working.

## FAQ

**What is environmental monitoring in a food factory?**  
A planned programme of sampling surfaces, drains, equipment and other parts of the processing environment, testing those samples for target organisms or hygiene indicators, and acting on the results. Codex treats it as a verification activity, not as HACCP monitoring.

**Which food factories need an environmental monitoring programme?**  
Any plant where the environment could contaminate food, and especially plants making ready-to-eat foods that are exposed after a kill step and before packing. Codex CXG 61 says the need is highest for ready-to-eat foods that support Listeria growth and get no post-packaging treatment. EAS 39 requires environmental sampling at a level commensurate with the risk.

**Is environmental monitoring a legal requirement in Kenya?**  
Kenya's food hygiene regulations require microbiological and other testing procedures to be used where necessary to identify sanitation failures or contamination, and EAS 39 requires environmental sampling and records of environmental tests. Check your obligations with KEBS, your county public health office and your certification body.

**What should a food factory swab for?**  
It depends on the product and process. Listeria spp. are a common indicator in wet, chilled ready-to-eat areas, Salmonella is often the concern in dry plants, and hygiene indicators such as TVC, Enterobacteriaceae or ATP are used to verify cleaning. Agree targets with a microbiologist or competent laboratory.

**How often should environmental samples be taken?**  
Codex says frequency should be based on the product, the process and the history of results, and increased after positive findings. Exposed food contact surfaces in ready-to-eat areas are normally sampled more often than areas further from the product.

**What should happen after a positive environmental result?**  
Follow your written response plan: intensified cleaning, extra sampling around the site to trace the source, review of hygiene practices and, where the product could have been affected, holding and testing it. Record the root cause and the corrective action, then re-sample.
