A lorry is waiting at the gate. The customer wants the order today. The store clerk can see pallets of finished goods on the floor, and the driver is already asking for the delivery note. In that moment, nobody wants to hear "wait, has QA cleared this batch?"

That question is exactly the one that protects your brand. A batch that leaves the warehouse with a failed test, a wrong expiry date or a torn seal becomes a complaint, a return, or in the worst case a withdrawal from the market. Catching it at the warehouse door costs minutes. Catching it at a supermarket shelf costs far more.

This article is for store managers, QA leads and owners of small and mid-sized food and agro factories in Kenya: dairies, millers, juice and edible-oil processors, bakeries, honey and spice packers. It gives you a simple seven-question checklist to run before any batch is released for dispatch. It is an operations guide, not legal advice.

## Why a release step matters

Most small factories have some form of quality checking. The weak point is usually not the test itself. It is the hand-off between the lab bench and the store. A result sits in a notebook, a WhatsApp message or a supervisor's head, while the stock sits on a pallet looking exactly like every other pallet.

Kenyan regulators and standards bodies expect manufacturers to know what they made and to control it. KEBS works on product standards and conformity under the Standards Act, and sector rules such as the dairy food safety regulations administered with the Kenya Dairy Board expect product to be identifiable by batch. Across food safety practice generally, product that does not conform may need to be held, reworked, downgraded or withdrawn. You do not need to memorise any regulation to act on the common thread: **every batch should be identifiable, and nothing should ship until someone with authority has said it can.**

A formal "QA hold" gives you that control. Finished goods go into a hold status when they are produced. They move to "released" only after the checks below pass. Until then, they are not available for sale, no matter how urgent the order.

## The 7-question QA hold checklist

Run these questions for each batch (or lot) before you allow it onto a delivery note. Keep the answers written down, on paper or in a system, so you can show them later.

### 1. Do we know exactly which batch this is?

Every pallet, carton or bag in the release should carry a batch or lot number that matches your production record. Check:

- The batch number on the product matches the batch number on the production sheet.
- The production date, shift and line are recorded against that batch.
- No unlabelled or mixed-batch cartons are hiding in the pallet.

If you cannot identify a carton, you cannot release it. Set it aside until you can.

### 2. Has QC actually passed this batch?

"We usually pass" is not a result. Confirm that the tests your process requires for this product have been done and recorded as passed. Depending on your product, that might mean moisture for flour, fat and acidity checks for milk products, Brix and pH for juice, or microbiology results where your plan requires them.

Ask:

- Are all required results recorded, with the date and the name of the person who did them?
- Are any results pending, such as lab work sent to an external laboratory?
- Did any result fall outside your specification, and if so, what was decided?

A batch with pending results stays on hold.

### 3. Is the labelling and date-marking correct?

Labelling errors are one of the most common reasons product is pulled back. The East African general standard for labelling prepackaged foods (EAS 38) and your product's own standard set out what a label should carry. On the warehouse floor, check the basics:

- Product name and pack size are correct for this batch.
- Batch number and production date are printed and legible.
- The "best before" or "use by" date is correct for the shelf life you have validated, not copied from yesterday's run.
- Any required marks or statements for your product are present.

Pick a few units from different cartons, not just the top layer.

### 4. Is the packaging intact?

A perfect product in a damaged pack is a damaged product. Before release, inspect a sample for:

- Torn sachets, leaking pouches, swollen or dented containers.
- Broken seals or caps that are not fully closed.
- Wet, crushed or stained cartons that suggest a spill or poor storage.

If you find damage, decide whether the affected units are removed and the rest released, or whether the whole batch needs a closer look. Record the decision.

### 5. Does the quantity match the order and the stock record?

Quality is not only about the product. It is also about sending the right amount of the right thing. Check:

- The quantity being released matches the production record minus any samples, rejects or rework.
- The quantity on the delivery note matches what is physically loaded.
- The batch being sent follows your stock rotation rule (for most perishable goods, first-expiry-first-out).

Mismatches here are often the first sign of an earlier problem: a miscount on the line, an unrecorded reject, or the wrong pallet picked.

### 6. Have all holds and quarantines been cleared?

Sometimes a batch is placed on hold for a reason outside routine testing: a customer complaint about a related batch, a raw material problem from a supplier, an equipment fault during the run, or a pest sighting in the store. Before release:

- Check whether any open hold or quarantine applies to this batch or to the raw materials used in it.
- Confirm the hold was closed by the right person, with a reason.
- Make sure quarantined stock is physically separated and clearly marked, not just "known about."

A hold that only exists in someone's memory is not a hold.

### 7. Who released it, and can we prove it?

The final question is about accountability. Every release should record:

- Who approved the release (by name and role).
- The date and time of release.
- Which checks were done and any exceptions accepted.

This document trail is what you will reach for if a customer, auditor or regulator asks about the batch later. It is also the starting point of any traceability exercise or mock recall.

## Make the checklist hard to skip

A checklist only works if the busy day cannot bypass it. A few practical habits help:

- **Default to hold.** New finished goods start in "on hold" status. Release is an active decision, not the default.
- **Separate physically.** Use a marked hold area or coloured tags for held stock, so pickers cannot grab it by accident.
- **Name the releasers.** Agree who can release product (for example, the QA lead and one deputy). Others cannot.
- **Link release to dispatch.** The delivery note should only be issued for released batches. If your store can issue a delivery note for held stock, the hold is decorative.
- **Review exceptions weekly.** Look at any batches released with exceptions, or held for a long time, and ask why.

**Illustration (not real data):** imagine a juice packer whose QA lead is off sick. Without a hold status, the store clerk sees pallets on the floor and loads them for a waiting distributor, unaware that the Brix result for one batch was outside specification. With a hold status linked to stock, that batch simply does not appear as available to dispatch, so the clerk cannot pick it, however urgent the order.

## FAQ

**What is a QA hold in a food factory?**
A QA hold is a status that keeps a batch of finished goods (or raw materials) from being used or sold until quality checks are complete and someone with authority releases it.

**Who should be allowed to release a batch?**
Usually the QA lead or a named deputy. The key point is that release authority is limited to specific people and every release is recorded with their name and the time.

**What should happen to a batch that fails a check?**
It stays on hold while you decide on the next step, which may include rework, downgrading, destruction or, if it has already left, withdrawal. Record the decision and the reason. For regulated products, follow the guidance of the relevant authority.

**Can a small factory run a QA hold without software?**
Yes. A marked hold area, a simple release log and a rule that delivery notes are only issued for released batches will cover the basics. Software becomes useful when batches and orders grow and paper records drift from physical stock.
