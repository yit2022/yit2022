*By Isaac Muthui, CEO, Tamalaki Business Network Ltd. Originally published at* [*factoraerp.com*](https://factoraerp.com/en/blog/cold-chain-temperature-monitoring-checklist-food-factories-kenya)*.*

Most food factories with a cold room can show you a temperature sheet. It usually has one reading in the morning and one in the afternoon, written in the same pen, and every number sits neatly inside the limit. What it cannot show you is what happened at 2 a.m. when the power went off, how warm the pallet next to the door got while the truck was loading, or whether anyone checked the yoghurt that sat on the dock for forty minutes before it went into the chiller.

That gap matters because temperature failures are quiet. Chilled and frozen products rarely look different after a few hours too warm. The damage shows up later, as shorter shelf life, spoiled stock, a customer complaint or, at worst, people falling ill. And when you are asked what happened, a sheet with two tidy readings a day is not evidence of control.

This cold chain temperature monitoring checklist is written for dairies, meat and fish processors, frozen vegetable and juice plants, and any Kenyan or East African food factory that stores or ships chilled or frozen product. It does not replace your HACCP plan, KEBS requirements or the Kenya Dairy Board. It sets out ten practical checks that turn temperature from a number on a wall into a controlled, recorded process, from the intake dock to the delivery truck.

## What the standards actually say

**The general rule.** The [Codex General Principles of Food Hygiene, CXC 1-1969](https://doi.org/10.4060/cc6125en), says that inadequate time and temperature control during cooking, cooling, processing and storage is "among the most common failures of operational control" (section 13.2.1). It asks for systems that specify "tolerable limits for time and temperature variations", and says temperature control systems that affect food safety "should be validated, and, as appropriate, monitored and recorded", with monitoring and recording devices "checked for accuracy and calibrated at regular intervals". Its HACCP annex adds that monitoring frequency should take into account the nature of the deviation, giving "a gradual increase in temperature in cold storage" as an example, and that "where possible, monitoring of CCPs should be continuous". At intake, it lists "temperature measurement for refrigerated and frozen foods" as one of the checks before processing (section 13.2.8).

**The East African code.** [EAS 39:2001, the East African code of practice for hygiene in the food and drink manufacturing industry](https://law.resource.org/pub/eac/ibr/eas.39.2001.pdf), says that "the maintenance of the cold chain is also critical to assure the safety of chilled and frozen" foods, and that foods kept at temperatures other than ambient "are most susceptible to abuse during distribution and retailing". Food that needs special storage conditions "shall be stored under the appropriate conditions, and records of the storage conditions shall be maintained" (8.2.2.3). Before loading, the refrigeration unit on a vehicle shall be inspected to make sure it works and "that there is adequate fuel in the units' fuel tank" (8.2.3.1). Food must be at the correct temperature when it is loaded, because refrigeration units on vehicles "have not the capacity to cool product" (8.2.3.4). Refrigerated transport should have a temperature recorder (8.3.2 and 8.3.4), and records of temperature "during production, storage and distribution" are listed among the records a factory keeps (14.3.4).

**Frozen food.** The [Codex Code of Practice for the Processing and Handling of Quick Frozen Foods, CXC 8-1976](https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/s3/2023-12/CXP-CAC-RCP8.pdf), is the most specific text. Quick frozen food should be kept at **\-18°C or colder** at all points in the cold chain, subject to permitted tolerances. During distribution any rise should be kept to a minimum and "should not in any case be warmer than -12ºC in the warmest package", and the product brought back to -18°C as soon as possible after delivery (4.7). Before freezing, the time spent "between 10°C and 60°C" should be "as short as possible" (4.2). It asks for "a contingency plan" to maintain product temperature "in the case of power losses or equipment failure" (3.1.5). When a load is too warm, it should be "identified and sorted immediately", and delivery and sale suspended while safety and quality are assessed (5.3). And records should be kept "for a period that exceeds the shelf-life of the product" (5.4).

**Dairy in Kenya.** The [Dairy Industry (Dairy Produce Safety) Regulations, 2021](https://new.kenyalaw.org/akn/ke/act/ln/2021/22/eng@2022-12-31) (Legal Notice 22 of 2021) require milk dispenser operators to keep dairy produce "between 4°C to 6°C at all times and records of this maintained" (regulation 71(o)), and operators of dispensing equipment to cool processed dairy produce to between 4°C and 7°C, fit storage containers with an indicating thermometer and "maintain regular temperature monitoring records" (regulation 81). Thermometers, "including Indicating and Recording", are on the list of equipment that needs a valid calibration certificate (regulation 98 and Form B). Those clauses are written for milk dispensing, not processing plants, but they show plainly what a regulator expects: a set range, a working thermometer and a record.

**Why it matters in Kenya.** A Kenyan cold chain runs on electricity, and electricity is not guaranteed. The nationwide blackout of 25 August 2023 "lasted more than 20 hours with on and off supply in some regions", and a Mombasa fish trader told the Daily Nation he had lost fish worth more than Sh300,000 ([Daily Nation](https://nation.africa/kenya/business/businesses-record-huge-losses-following-blackout-4348824)). The Fresh Produce Consortium of Kenya estimated its members' losses "in the hundreds of millions" of shillings, its chief executive saying "we have cold chains which run on power and as it is, they cannot be used" ([Daily Nation](https://nation.africa/kenya/business/fresh-produce-exporters-suffer-multiple-hits-from-power-outage-4348538)). A factory cannot prevent a grid failure. It can decide in advance what happens to its product when one occurs.

## Why a thermometer on the wall is not enough

A wall thermometer measures the air at one point. The product in the far corner, on the top shelf near the evaporator or on the pallet beside the door can be several degrees different. Codex makes the same distinction between air temperature monitoring and product temperature monitoring, and notes that a non-destructive reading between packages can differ from the true product temperature by up to 2°C.

A reading twice a day also misses most of what goes wrong. Excursions tend to happen at night, during defrost cycles, during long loading sessions, after a door is left open, or when a compressor trips and nobody notices until morning. And a reading on its own decides nothing. The control is what happens next: who is told, what is checked, which stock is held, and who decides whether it can still be sold.

## The 10-step cold chain temperature monitoring checklist

### 1\. Set temperature limits for every product and location, in writing

- List every chilled and frozen product and every place it sits: intake dock, raw material chiller, process area, blast freezer, finished goods cold room, dispatch area and vehicles.
- For each, write the target, the upper limit and the tolerance: how far above the limit, for how long, before it counts as an excursion. Base them on your HACCP plan, the product standard, your customer specifications and, for frozen food, the Codex -18°C rule.
- Agree the same limits with your transporters and distributors, so everyone in the chain is working to the same numbers.

### 2\. Map your cold areas and place sensors where the risk is

- Find the warm spots in each cold room or freezer, empty and full: near doors, near the evaporator fans, at the top of high racks, at the back of a fully loaded room. A simple mapping exercise with several loggers over a few days will show you.
- Place fixed sensors where they represent the stored product, not where they are convenient. Codex recommends air sensors "placed high up" and away from fans and doors, and gives indicative numbers: one sensor for cold stores under 500 m³, more for larger rooms.
- Record where each sensor is, so a reading can always be tied to a location.

### 3\. Calibrate and check every thermometer and logger

- Keep a register of every fixed sensor, data logger and probe thermometer, with its location, range and calibration due date.
- Check probes against a reference regularly, for example in an ice bath, and calibrate on schedule. Codex suggests air recorders accurate to within ±2°C and product probes to ±0.5°C. For dairies, the 2021 regulations require a calibration certificate for indicating and recording thermometers. Our [calibration checklist](https://factoraerp.com/en/blog/calibration-checklist-food-factories-kenya) covers the routine.
- Take any instrument that fails its check out of use, and review the readings it gave since its last good check.

### 4\. Check temperature at receiving, before anything goes in the store

- Measure and record the product temperature of every chilled or frozen delivery, and of raw milk at the intake bay, against the lot number. Codex notes that for highly perishable products, temperature at receiving may be a critical control point.
- Look at the delivery before you probe it: signs of thawing and refreezing, wet or crushed cartons, ice crystals, warm loads. Ask for the vehicle's temperature record.
- Decide in writing what happens to a warm delivery: accept, reject or hold for assessment. Our [dairy milk intake checklist](https://factoraerp.com/en/blog/dairy-milk-intake-checklist-kenya) and [supplier intake checklist](https://factoraerp.com/en/blog/supplier-intake-checklist-food-manufacturers-kenya) cover the wider intake routine.

### 5\. Control time out of refrigeration during processing

- Set and record maximum times for product to sit outside the cold room: on the dock, in staging, waiting for the line, between processing steps. Codex says time in the "critical temperature zone" between 10°C and 60°C should be "as short as possible".
- Where product is cooked or pasteurised and then cooled, set and record the cooling time and end temperature as part of the batch record.
- Bring product out in batches the line can use, rather than a whole pallet at once.

### 6\. Run cold rooms with discipline

- Do not overload. Keep air channels between pallets and away from walls so cold air can reach the product, as Codex expects for frozen storage.
- Keep doors closed, use strip curtains where they help, and do not leave doors propped open during loading. Plan defrost cycles outside busy periods.
- Maintain the room itself: door seals, insulation, ice build-up, drainage and refrigerant leaks. Put compressors and condensers on your [preventive maintenance](https://factoraerp.com/en/blog/preventive-maintenance-basics-food-agro-factories-kenya) plan, and rotate stock first expired, first out, as in our [FEFO and expiry tracking guide](https://factoraerp.com/en/blog/reduce-expiry-spoilage-losses-fefo-batch-expiry-tracking).

### 7\. Monitor continuously, and make alarms reach a person

- Use recording loggers or a monitoring system that reads every few minutes, around the clock. Keep manual readings as a cross-check, not as the only record.
- Set alarms on the limits and tolerances from step 1, and decide who receives them at night and at weekends, how quickly they must respond, and who they escalate to if they cannot.
- Treat a missing reading as an alarm. A logger with a flat battery or a sensor that has stopped reporting is a gap in your evidence.

### 8\. Plan for power cuts and equipment failure

- Write the contingency plan Codex asks for: what happens if the grid goes down, if the generator fails to start, or if a compressor breaks.
- Test the standby generator under load on a schedule, keep fuel above a set minimum, and record both. Check that the generator actually powers the cold rooms and the monitoring system, not only the lights.
- Agree in advance: keep doors shut, which product moves first and where, who can authorise moving stock to another cold store, and when a room's contents must be assessed. A long outage like August 2023 is the wrong moment to invent the plan.

### 9\. Load and ship cold

- Pre-cool vehicles before loading, as Codex recommends, and check that the vehicle is clean and that the refrigeration unit works and has fuel, as EAS 39 requires.
- Load only product that is already at the correct temperature; the vehicle cannot cool it down. Record product temperature at loading and keep the vehicle's temperature record, as EAS 39 recommends. Our [finished goods dispatch checklist](https://factoraerp.com/en/blog/finished-goods-dispatch-checklist-kenya) covers the rest of the dispatch routine.
- For multi-drop routes, plan the order of deliveries and limit door openings. Codex says frozen product should never be warmer than -12°C in the warmest package during distribution.

### 10\. Handle every excursion: hold, assess, decide, record

- When a reading goes outside the tolerance, identify the affected stock immediately and stop it from being sold or used. Codex says such loads should be "identified and sorted immediately" and delivery and sale suspended.
- Put it on a real stock hold, as in our [QA hold checklist](https://factoraerp.com/en/blog/qa-hold-checklist-before-batch-leaves-warehouse-kenya), so it cannot be dispatched by mistake. Then assess it: how warm, for how long, what the product is, and what your HACCP plan and shelf-life data say.
- Record the decision, who made it, and the corrective action on the equipment or the routine. Inform the supplier or customer where the stock has already moved, and the authorities where safety is compromised, as Codex advises. If product has already left, a [mock recall](https://factoraerp.com/en/blog/how-to-run-a-mock-recall-food-traceability-kenya) is how you prove you can find it.

## The daily cold chain record

At the end of each day, a factory with chilled or frozen product should be able to answer five questions from records, not memory:

1. What were the minimum and maximum temperatures in each cold room, freezer and vehicle, and were there any gaps in the readings?
2. Which deliveries were received, at what product temperature, and were any rejected or held?
3. Were there any excursions or alarms, how long did they last, who responded, and how quickly?
4. Which batches or lots were affected, what was decided about each, and who decided?
5. Which instruments were checked or calibrated, and are any overdue?

If any answer depends on one person's notebook, that is the gap to close first.

## Habits that make cold chain control stick

- **Review the charts, not just the sheet.** A weekly look at temperature trends shows a compressor working harder, a door seal failing or a room that warms every afternoon, before it becomes an excursion.
- **Make it safe to report.** The night guard who notices the freezer alarm, or the loader who sees a warm pallet, needs a clear number to call and thanks for calling it.
- **Count what temperature failures cost.** Record what is written off or downgraded after each excursion. It turns generator fuel and maintenance from a cost into an easy decision.
- **Close the loop with transporters.** Share their temperature records with them and agree what happens to a warm load, before the first dispute.

## FAQ

**What should a cold chain temperature monitoring checklist include?**\
Written limits and tolerances per product and location, mapped sensor positions, calibrated thermometers and loggers, temperature checks at receiving, limits on time out of refrigeration, cold room discipline, continuous monitoring with alarms that reach a person, a power failure plan, temperature checks at loading, and a documented procedure for excursions.

**What temperature should frozen food be kept at?**\
Codex CXC 8-1976 says quick frozen food should be kept at -18°C or colder at all points in the cold chain, subject to permitted tolerances, and never warmer than -12°C in the warmest package during distribution. Check the standard for your product and any KEBS or customer requirements.

**What temperature should milk be kept at in Kenya?**\
The Dairy Industry (Dairy Produce Safety) Regulations, 2021 require milk dispensers to keep dairy produce between 4°C and 6°C with records, and dispensing equipment operators to cool processed dairy produce to between 4°C and 7°C. Processors should set their own limits from their HACCP plan, the relevant KEBS/EAS standard and Kenya Dairy Board requirements.

**How often should cold room temperatures be recorded?**\
Codex says CCP monitoring should be continuous where possible, and frequent enough to detect a deviation in time to isolate the affected product. In practice that means a recording logger or monitoring system that reads every few minutes, with manual checks as a cross-check.

**What should we do after a temperature excursion?**\
Identify and hold the affected stock immediately, assess how warm it got and for how long against your HACCP plan, decide whether it can be released, reprocessed or must be destroyed, record who decided and why, fix the cause, and inform customers or authorities where needed.

**How long should temperature records be kept?**\
Codex CXC 8-1976 says records should be kept for a period that exceeds the shelf life of the product, or as required by the competent authority. Keeping them at least that long lets you answer a complaint about any batch still on the market.
