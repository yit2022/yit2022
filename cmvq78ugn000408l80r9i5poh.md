*By Isaac Muthui, CEO, Tamalaki Business Network Ltd. Originally published at [factoraerp.com](https://factoraerp.com/en/blog/food-safety-training-checklist-food-factories-kenya).*

Ask most food factories whether their staff are trained and the answer is yes. There is a file with attendance sheets. Somebody ran a hygiene talk in March. The new casuals were shown around on their first morning. The HACCP team went on a course two years ago.

Then a CCP check is done the wrong way, a cleaner mixes a chemical by guesswork, or a supervisor waves through a batch that should have been held, and the file of attendance sheets turns out to prove very little. People sat in a room. Nobody checked whether they could do the job.

This food safety training checklist is written for dairies, millers, bakeries, juice, snack, edible oil and other Kenyan and East African food and agro factories. It does not replace your HACCP team, your certification body, your county public health officer or the guidance of your competent authority. It sets out ten practical checks for a training programme that produces competent people, and records that show it.

## What the standards actually say

**Codex.** The [Codex General Principles of Food Hygiene, CXC 1-1969](https://doi.org/10.4060/cc6125en), gives training its own section (section 10, "Training and competence"). Its objective is that everyone who comes "directly or indirectly into contact with food should have sufficient understanding of food hygiene to ensure they have competence appropriate to the operations they are to perform". It says training needs depend on the hazards of the food, how it is produced and packed, how it is stored and how long before it is eaten, and on "the use and maintenance of instruments and equipment". It adds that "those who handle cleaning chemicals or other potentially hazardous chemicals should be instructed in proper use to prevent contamination of food" (10.1).

Two lines in Codex matter most for this checklist. First: "Periodic assessments of the effectiveness of training and instruction programmes should be made, as well as routine supervision and verification to ensure that procedures are being carried out effectively" (10.3). Second: systems should ensure that food handlers and other personnel, "such as maintenance staff, remain aware of all procedures", and "records should be kept of training activities" (10.4). For HACCP, Codex says working instructions should define "the tasks of the operating personnel in charge of each CCP", that training should be pitched at the trainee's level, and that "re-training may be needed as part of corrective actions for some deviations" (19.13). It also says ongoing HACCP training "is necessary for all levels of personnel, including managers".

**The East African code.** [EAS 39:2001, the East African code of practice for hygiene in the food and drink manufacturing industry](https://law.resource.org/pub/eac/ibr/eas.39.2001.pdf), is short and direct: "All staff including management staff shall be given appropriate induction and on going training in hygiene. This training shall be carried out by personnel who are competent in this field" (5.3.1), and "Training records shall be kept" (5.3.2). It also requires that "all cleaning personnel shall be well trained in cleaning techniques".

**Kenya's food hygiene regulations.** Under the [Food, Drugs and Chemical Substances (Food Hygiene) Regulations](https://faolex.fao.org/docs/pdf/ken106527.pdf) made under Cap. 254, regulation 15(1) requires whoever runs a food plant to take reasonable measures to ensure that "the personnel responsible for identifying sanitation failures or food contamination are properly trained to provide a level of competency necessary for the production of clean and safe food", that food handlers and supervisors are trained in "proper techniques and food protection principles", and that responsibility for compliance is "assigned to competent supervisory personnel". The same regulation requires medical examination of employees before employment and at intervals of not more than twelve months, with health certificates kept at the plant.

**Kenya's occupational safety law.** The [Occupational Safety and Health Act, 2007](https://www.labour.go.ke/sites/default/files/2022-10/Occupational-Safety-and-Health-Act-2007.pdf) is not a food law, but it applies to every factory floor. Section 6(2)(c) makes the occupier responsible for "such information, instruction, training and supervision as is necessary" for safety and health at work. Section 99 says nobody should work at a machine or process liable to cause injury unless fully instructed and either trained or adequately supervised, and that this training must happen on recruitment, on transfer or change of job, when new equipment or materials are introduced and when new technology is introduced. It must be "repeated periodically", and contractors working on site must receive appropriate instructions too. Those trigger points are a useful model for food safety training as well.

**Dairy.** The [Dairy Industry (Dairy Produce Safety) Regulations, 2021](https://new.kenyalaw.org/akn/ke/act/ln/2021/22/eng@2022-12-31) require, among the conditions for milk dispensers, that the equipment "is operated only by persons trained to handle dairy produce": a reminder that the regulator expects training to be tied to specific tasks.

**A useful benchmark.** US rules are not binding in Kenya, but they are explicit. [21 CFR 117.4](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-117/subpart-A/section-117.4) requires every person who manufactures, processes, packs or holds food, "including temporary and seasonal personnel", to have the education, training or experience needed for their assigned duties and to receive training in food hygiene and food safety, "including the importance of employee health and personal hygiene". Supervisors must be qualified to supervise, and training records "must be established and maintained".

## Why training programmes go wrong

Training rarely fails because a factory does none. It fails because the programme measures attendance, not ability.

The patterns are familiar. Casual and seasonal workers start on the line before anyone has shown them the basics, because the order is urgent. Everyone gets the same general hygiene talk, while the person who checks pasteuriser temperatures or the metal detector gets no task-specific training at all. Training is a one-off at induction and never repeated. Maintenance technicians, contractors and drivers are left out. Records show who attended but not who passed. And when something goes wrong, the corrective action says "operator retrained", with no record of what was taught or whether it worked.

## The 10-step food safety training checklist

### 1. Build a training matrix by role and task

- List every role in the plant (intake, stores, mixing, processing, CCP operators, packing, cleaning, maintenance, QA, dispatch, drivers, supervisors, managers) against the training each one needs.
- Base it on the hazards, as Codex suggests: a person handling allergens, chemicals or a CCP needs more than a person moving sealed cartons.
- Mark which training is required before someone may work unsupervised, and which can follow within a set period.

### 2. Induct everyone before their first shift, including casuals

- No one enters a production area without a basic induction: handwashing, protective clothing, illness reporting, jewellery and personal items, where they may eat and smoke, and what to do if they see a problem. Our [personal hygiene and staff health checklist](https://factoraerp.com/en/blog/personal-hygiene-staff-health-checklist-food-factories-kenya) covers the content.
- Keep a short, picture-based induction for casual and seasonal workers, in the language they understand best. Peak season is exactly when induction gets skipped.
- Check medical certificates before the first shift, not after.

### 3. Train CCP operators on written work instructions

- Codex asks for working instructions that define the tasks of the people in charge of each CCP. Write them as short, step-by-step instructions with the critical limit, how to check it, how to record it and what to do if it fails.
- Train each CCP operator on their specific instruction, at the line, and sign them off only when they can do it unaided. Our [HACCP CCP monitoring checklist](https://factoraerp.com/en/blog/haccp-ccp-monitoring-checklist-food-factories-kenya) explains what a good CCP record looks like.
- Keep a list of who is authorised for each CCP, and make sure every shift has at least one.

### 4. Instruct cleaning and chemical handlers properly

- Codex singles out people who handle cleaning chemicals and other hazardous chemicals; EAS 39 says cleaning staff "shall be well trained in cleaning techniques".
- Cover dilution, contact time, rinsing, which chemical goes where, labelling of decanted containers and what to do after a spill. See our [cleaning and sanitation checklist](https://factoraerp.com/en/blog/cleaning-sanitation-checklist-food-factories-kenya) and [chemical control checklist](https://factoraerp.com/en/blog/chemical-control-checklist-food-factories-kenya).
- Cover machine safety too: under section 99 of the OSHA, people working on machines must be trained or adequately supervised.

### 5. Train supervisors and managers to spot and act on deviations

- Codex says managers and supervisors should know enough to "identify deviations and take necessary action". Kenya's food hygiene regulations assign compliance responsibility to competent supervisory personnel.
- Supervisors need training in the HACCP plan, when to stop a line, when to put product on hold and how to escalate. Managers need enough to resource the system and review it.
- EAS 39 is explicit that management staff need training too. Do not exempt the people who sign off the batches.

### 6. Include maintenance staff, contractors, drivers and visitors

- Codex names maintenance staff specifically. Technicians open equipment, use lubricants and tools, and leave parts behind. Train them in hygiene, food-grade lubricants, tool control and handing equipment back clean.
- Brief contractors and visitors before they enter production areas, and record that they were briefed. The OSHA requires contractors to receive appropriate instructions on site.
- Drivers and dispatch staff need the basics of load hygiene and temperature control for the products they move.

### 7. Test competence, not attendance

- An attendance sheet shows that someone was in the room. Competence means they can do the task. Sign people off by watching them do it: "Show me how you check the temperature", "Show me how you mix this sanitiser".
- Use short practical checks or a few oral questions instead of long written tests, especially for workers with limited literacy.
- Record who assessed them, when and the result. Someone who does not pass is retrained and re-assessed, not signed off anyway.

### 8. Set refresher and retraining triggers

- Refresh training on a schedule (for example yearly for hygiene, and more often for CCP operators), as Codex and the OSHA both expect training to be repeated.
- Retrain on change: new product, new line, new equipment, new chemical, new allergen, change of job. These are the OSHA section 99 trigger points, and they fit food safety equally well.
- Retrain after deviations where the cause was a skill gap, as Codex suggests. But do not use "retrained" as a lazy root cause: if the procedure or equipment is the real problem, fix that.

### 9. Keep training records that answer questions quickly

- For each person, keep what they were trained in, when, by whom, how they were assessed, the result and when the next refresher is due.
- Keep medical certificates, food handler certificates and any other required certificates with their expiry dates, and check them before rostering.
- File records where QA and auditors can find them in minutes, not in a box in the HR office.

### 10. Check that training works, and update it

- Codex asks for periodic assessments of the effectiveness of training. Look at what happens on the floor: internal audit findings, CCP deviations, complaints, near misses and observation results by area and shift.
- If the same mistake keeps appearing, the training (or the procedure behind it) needs to change. Our [internal audit checklist](https://factoraerp.com/en/blog/internal-audit-checklist-food-factories-kenya) explains how to watch the work instead of just reading records.
- Review the training matrix at least once a year and after every significant change.

## The training record

For each person, the record should let a supervisor answer six questions without searching:

| Question | What the record should show |
|---|---|
| What does this person do? | Role, area and the tasks or CCPs they perform |
| What training does the role require? | Items from the training matrix, marked "before working unsupervised" or "within X days" |
| What have they completed? | Topic, date, trainer and the version of the procedure taught |
| Can they actually do it? | Assessment method (observed, oral, practical), assessor, result and date |
| Are their certificates valid? | Medical and food handler certificates, and other required certificates, with expiry dates |
| What is due next? | Refresher dates, retraining after changes or deviations, and any open actions |

If any answer is "the supervisor knows", the training programme is not yet under control.

## An illustrative example

*The following scenario is illustrative only. It is not based on a specific factory.*

A juice plant takes on twelve seasonal workers for the mango season. Under time pressure, they are shown the changing room and put on the filling line. One of them is asked to cover the pasteuriser temperature check during a lunch break. He records the reading from the display, not the calibrated thermometer, because nobody told him there was a difference. The deviation is found two days later during a record review, and the affected batches have to be held and assessed.

With a training matrix, the pasteuriser check would have been marked as "authorised operators only", the roster would have shown that no authorised person was covering lunch, and the seasonal worker would have had a short induction before his first shift. None of that requires an expensive course. It requires knowing who is trained to do what.

## Habits that make training stick

- **Train at the line.** Five minutes at the machine beats an hour in a meeting room.
- **Show, then watch.** Demonstrate the task, then watch the person do it before signing them off.
- **Short and often.** Brief toolbox talks at shift start keep the basics fresh.
- **Pictures over paragraphs.** Visual work instructions at the point of use help workers with different languages and literacy levels.
- **Link training to findings.** Every repeat audit finding or deviation is a prompt to ask whether the training, the procedure or the equipment needs to change.

## FAQ

**What should a food safety training checklist include?**  
A training matrix by role and task, induction before the first shift (including casual and seasonal workers), task-specific training for CCP operators, instruction for cleaning and chemical handlers, training for supervisors and managers, briefings for maintenance staff, contractors and visitors, competence assessment, refresher and retraining triggers, training records with certificate expiry dates, and regular checks that the training works.

**Is food safety training a legal requirement in Kenya?**  
Yes. Kenya's Food, Drugs and Chemical Substances (Food Hygiene) Regulations require food plant operators to ensure staff responsible for identifying sanitation failures or contamination are properly trained, that food handlers and supervisors are trained in food protection principles, and that employees are medically examined at least every twelve months. The Occupational Safety and Health Act, 2007 also requires instruction, training and supervision for safety at work. EAS 39 requires induction and ongoing hygiene training and training records.

**How often should food factory staff be retrained?**  
Codex says training programmes should be routinely reviewed and that systems should keep staff aware of procedures. Many plants refresh general hygiene training at least yearly, train CCP operators more often, and retrain whenever there is a new product, line, equipment, chemical or change of job, and after deviations caused by a skill gap.

**Do casual and seasonal workers need food safety training?**  
Yes. EAS 39 requires appropriate induction for all staff, and Codex covers everyone who comes directly or indirectly into contact with food. The US benchmark rule, 21 CFR 117.4, explicitly includes temporary and seasonal personnel. At minimum, casual workers need a basic hygiene induction and a valid medical certificate before their first shift.

**What records should be kept for food safety training?**  
For each person: what training they received, when, from whom, how they were assessed, the result, and when refresher training is due, plus medical and food handler certificates with expiry dates. EAS 39 and Codex both require training records.

**How do you know if food safety training is working?**  
Watch the work and look at the results: internal audit findings, CCP deviations, complaints and near misses by area and shift. If the same mistakes keep happening, the training, the procedure or the equipment needs to change.
